# Apollo's training deck

Flashcard content for the board's Training tab. This is Apollo teaching, not
Apollo grading -- same brain, same judgment (coaching/METHODOLOGY.md's Core
judgment governs what counts as an objection, addressed, overcome, or
assumptive; this file just turns that judgment into practice material for
new and seasoned producers).

**This file evolves WITH Core judgment and the techniques scorecard, not
separately from them** (Frank, 2026-09-11: "the training, coaching and
brain of apollo should evolve together"). When Frank's nightly feedback
changes what Apollo credits as addressed, overcome, assumptive, or a strong
technique, the cards below need the same update -- a flashcard that
contradicts what real calls actually get graded against is actively
misleading, not just stale. This runs both directions: METHODOLOGY.md's own
header now points back here for the same reason.

**Where the content comes from.** Not invented. Pulled from this agency's
own real coached calls (2026-09-03 through 2026-09-10's objection
categories, technique verdicts, and Apollo's own "fix" suggestions on real
transcripts), then consolidated into the recurring patterns and generalized
-- specific enough to be useful, general enough to not be a script to
recite word-for-word. Update a card the same way Core judgment gets
updated: when a new recurring pattern shows up that isn't covered yet, when
a card's advice stops matching what's actually working on real calls, or
when a technique that currently has "no strong example yet" finally gets
one on a graded call.

**Format.** `## Deck Name` starts a deck. `### Front text` starts a card;
everything until the next `###` or `##` is the back. site/worker.js parses
this by those two heading levels -- do not change the heading structure
without checking that parser.

## Insurance Basics

### Premium

What the customer pays (monthly, semi-annually, or annually) to keep a
policy in force. Not the same as a quote -- a quote is an offered premium;
it becomes "the premium" once the policy is bound.

### Deductible

The amount the customer pays out of pocket on a claim before the insurance
company pays the rest. Higher deductible = lower premium, and vice versa --
this tradeoff is the single most common lever in a price objection (see the
"Price higher than current" card in Objections).

### Liability coverage

Pays for the OTHER party's injuries or property damage when the insured is
at fault. Does not pay for the insured's own vehicle or injuries -- that's
what comprehensive/collision and medical payments/PIP are for. Every state
requires some minimum liability limit; most customers carry only that
minimum without realizing it.

### Bodily injury liability (BI) vs. Property damage liability (PD)

Two halves of liability coverage. BI pays for the other party's medical
bills/injuries; PD pays for damage to their car, fence, mailbox, etc. Quoted
as three numbers, e.g. "100/300/100" -- $100k per person injured, $300k per
accident, $100k in property damage.

### Comprehensive coverage

Pays for damage to the INSURED's own vehicle from something other than a
collision -- theft, fire, hail, a deer strike, a cracked windshield. Often
confused with "full coverage"; comprehensive alone does not cover a crash.

### Collision coverage

Pays for damage to the INSURED's own vehicle from a collision, regardless of
fault. Usually bought together with comprehensive. A financed or leased
vehicle almost always requires both (see the "Financed vehicle" card).

### "Full coverage"

Not a real, defined insurance term -- it's what customers call a policy that
includes liability + comprehensive + collision (and often more). Useful to
know because a customer who says "I have full coverage" may only mean
liability plus one of the other two. Always confirm what's actually on the
policy rather than assuming from the phrase.

### Uninsured / underinsured motorist coverage (UM/UIM)

Pays the insured when the AT-FAULT other driver has no insurance, or not
enough to cover the damage. Roughly 1 in 8 drivers nationally carries no
insurance at all -- this is a real, common gap, not a theoretical one.

### Umbrella policy

Extra liability coverage that sits on top of auto/home limits once those are
exhausted -- e.g., a $1M umbrella kicks in after a $300k auto liability
limit is used up in a serious accident. Usually requires the underlying auto
and home policies to carry minimum liability limits first.

### Endorsement

A change or addition to an existing policy without rewriting the whole
thing -- adding a driver, adding a rideshare endorsement, raising a
liability limit mid-term.

### SR-22

Not insurance itself -- a form the insurer files with the state certifying
the customer carries the state-required minimum liability coverage,
typically required after a serious violation (DUI, driving uninsured,
multiple at-fault accidents). The policy itself is ordinary auto insurance;
the SR-22 is paperwork on top of it.

### Named insured vs. additional driver

The named insured is the policyholder -- the person the policy is written
for. An additional driver is anyone else regularly driving the same
vehicle(s) who needs to be listed, even if they're not the one paying.
Missing an additional driver is a common reason a claim gets denied later.

### Actual cash value (ACV) vs. replacement cost

ACV pays what the damaged/destroyed item was worth right before the loss
(after depreciation) -- an older roof or an older car pays out at its
depreciated value. Replacement cost pays what it costs to replace it new,
no depreciation deducted. This distinction is the whole answer to "why did
my claim only pay X."

### Renewal date / X-date

The date an existing policy comes up for renewal (or, for a lead who was
quoted before but never bought, the date they said they'd be ready to
switch). Capturing this accurately is one of Apollo's own 9 call-structure
dimensions -- see coaching/METHODOLOGY.md -- because a switch usually only
makes financial sense timed to this date, to avoid a cancellation fee or a
short-rate penalty on the old policy.

### Underwriting

The insurance company's own process of deciding whether to accept a risk and
at what price -- driving record, claims history, credit-based insurance
score (where allowed), the vehicle or home itself. A producer doesn't
control underwriting, but accurate information up front (see "Discovery" in
the Getting the Right Information deck) is what keeps a quote from changing
after underwriting reviews it.

## Objections Apollo Sees Most

### "I'm already insured / already have coverage / just renewed"

By far the most common objection this agency's calls hit (11 of the last
several days' logged objections). It is NOT a reason to end the call -- it's
an opening for two real questions: their renewal date, and whether they're
actually happy with the rate. Real lines that worked:
- "Got it — when's that policy up for renewal? I'd love to make sure this
  quote is useful to you at the right time."
- "That's great to hear. Just curious, are you happy with your current
  rate, or would you want a second quote to compare?"
The failure mode Apollo keeps flagging: taking "I'm insured" as a full no
and closing the call with a throwaway acknowledgment, asking nothing.

### "Your price is higher than what I'm paying now"

Second most common. The producer's job is NOT to defend the number silently
or concede outright -- it's to find out exactly what they're comparing
against and reframe on deductible, coverage, or bundling. Real lines that
worked:
- "Can I ask what you're paying today so I can see exactly where the gap
  is?"
- "Would you be open to me bundling both policies together to see if that
  gets you under your current total?"
- "Before we call it, can I check what your current policy actually covers
  for [liability/water damage/etc.] — sometimes the cheaper premium is
  missing coverage you'd want."
The failure mode: conceding the loss outright with a vague promise ("maybe
next renewal it'll be better") instead of a concrete reframe right now.

### "I need to talk to my spouse first"

A real deferral, not a brush-off -- but it still has a next step. Real lines
that worked:
- "Would it help if I tried them right now on speakerphone so I can walk
  them through it myself?"
- "What time do you think you'll talk to them today? I can call back right
  after so we can lock it in together."
- "Before you go, is there any part of the coverage you'd want me to
  explain again for when you talk to them?"
The failure mode: acknowledging it and re-reciting the same numbers instead
of asking anything about the actual spousal conversation.

### "I want to think about it" / "let me call you back"

A soft brush-off. The fix is never accepting silence — it's naming the real
cost of waiting (a renewal date, an expiring rate lock) and proposing a
specific next contact, not an open-ended "call me." Real lines that worked:
- "It expires [date], so let's not risk waiting — can we do a quick
  10-minute call this Thursday to lock in your options?"
- "No problem — can I text you a quick summary so it's easy to pull up when
  you're free?"
The failure mode: answering only the literal question asked (e.g. an
expiration date) without ever proposing a concrete callback time.

### "I'm at work / bad timing / can't talk right now"

A scheduling objection, not a real no. Always negotiate a SPECIFIC callback
window before hanging up. Real lines that worked:
- "No problem — is later today around 5 or 6 better, or would tomorrow
  morning work?"
- "Totally understand — before I let you go, can I confirm your renewal
  date is still next month so I call back with the right info?"
The failure mode: saying "I'll call you back" with no time attached at all.

### "I'm not interested" (no reason given)

A vague objection is an invitation to find out what's actually behind it —
price, coverage, timing, or a bad past experience — not something to accept
at face value. Real lines that worked:
- "Can I ask what's driving that — is it the price, the coverage, or just
  bad timing?"
- "Totally understand — is it okay if I follow up with a comparison in
  writing so you can look it over on your own time?"
The failure mode: thanking them and ending the call with zero discovery
attempted.

### "My vehicle is financed, I can't change coverage" / "the bank requires this"

A real constraint, but usually a smaller one than the customer thinks —
lenders require full coverage (comprehensive + collision) and a minimum
liability limit, not the customer's EXACT current limits. There's almost
always room to raise liability limits (protecting the customer, not the
bank) without touching what the loan requires. Real line that worked: "The
loan requires full coverage, but that doesn't mean your liability limits
have to stay where they are — we can raise those without touching what the
bank requires."

## Getting the Right Information

Apollo's 9-dimension call-structure framework (see METHODOLOGY.md) is real-
call grading criteria, but the underlying habits are exactly what a producer
should be doing on every call, coached or not.

### Opening & identification

State who you are, what agency, and why you're calling, and confirm you're
speaking with the right person — BEFORE moving into any pitch. A pitch that
starts before identification lands loses the prospect's trust immediately,
even if everything after it is well done.

### Discovery

Ask about the household's actual situation before pitching: who they're
with now, what matters most to them (price vs. coverage vs. service), any
recent life change (new car, new driver, moved, new baby). A pitch recited
without asking anything first is a missed discovery, not just a weak one.

### Current premium captured

Get the ACTUAL number the prospect is paying now — never assume or
ballpark it. A quote or a price-objection response built on an assumed
premium falls apart the moment the real number comes out.

### Renewal / X-date captured

Get or confirm the date the current policy renews or expires. This is the
single most useful piece of information on the call — it's what makes "call
back closer to your renewal" a real plan instead of a stall.

### Bundle / cross-sell raised

Always ask about a product the household doesn't already have — home, life,
umbrella — as a real question, not a buried mention. This applies on
EVERY call, service calls included: a household you're already talking to
for any reason is still a household that might be missing a policy.

### Next step specificity

End every call with a concrete, dated next step — "I'll call you Thursday
at 3 with the final numbers" — never a vague "I'll follow up" or "I'll get
back to you." A vague next step is functionally the same as no next step.

## Sales Techniques

Apollo already scores real calls against these six named techniques (see
METHODOLOGY.md's "techniques" scorecard, added 2026-09-10). Unlike the
Objections deck above, this one is honest that the team has not yet
produced a real, fully-credited ("strong") example of most of these on a
graded call -- across every day processed so far, zero calls have scored
"s" on any of the six. That's the finding, not a data fault (see
METHODOLOGY.md's own note on this). Each card below gives the real
definition Apollo grades against, plus the closest real attempt on file
where one exists. Update this deck as real strong examples land on actual
calls -- that's the whole point of grounding training in live calls instead
of a generic script.

### Elevator pitch

A short, prepared reason-to-switch delivered in the first couple minutes,
BEFORE any pushback -- this agency's own script calls it the Benefit
Statement: "we've helped a lot of people in [city] switch... because we
have great rates, with better coverage." Only counts as strong if it lands
early and unprompted, not recited later as a reaction to an objection.
Closest real attempt on file (scored weak, not strong): "compare what you
have with what's available and see what makes the most sense to you" --
too generic, not tailored to what the prospect actually called about. Full
credit needs the SPECIFIC agency script, not a paraphrase of it.

### Feel-Felt-Found

Acknowledge the objection ("I understand how you feel"), normalize it
against other real clients ("a lot of people feel the same way when..."),
then resolve it with what those clients found or decided. All three steps,
in that order, are required for full credit -- an "I understand" with no
follow-through is weak at best. Closest real attempt on file: "mucha gente
piensa lo mismo que es con mucho trabajo, pero aquí con nosotros siempre lo
habíamos [resuelto]" -- normalizes the concern (the "felt" step) but never
lands a concrete "found" -- what those other clients actually decided or
discovered.

### Risk reversal

Removes the cost of saying yes right now -- "this won't cost you anything
today," a pre-approval framing, an easy first step -- tied to an actual
objection the prospect raised, not a generic reassurance floating free of
any concern. Closest real attempt on file: "if we're not beating the cost
of your current insurance or don't have better coverage, then we'll just
try again at renewal" -- a real risk-reversal framing, but delivered flatly
rather than tied to the specific price concern the prospect had just
raised. Full credit needs the same framing anchored explicitly to what the
prospect just said.

### Social proof

A specific other client or a real number -- not a bare claim like
"everyone loves us." No real attempt has been scored yet on any processed
day. This is a genuinely open gap: there is no example on file of a
producer citing a specific comparable client or a concrete satisfaction
number on a real call.

### Trial close

A small agreement check ("does that make sense?") placed to bank
agreement BEFORE the final ask -- not the final ask itself, and not empty
verbal filler ("you know?", "right?"). No real attempt has been scored yet
on any processed day.

### Takeaway / urgency

A REAL, specific reason to act now -- a rate that could change, a discount
tied to timing -- never a fabricated deadline. No real attempt has been
scored yet on any processed day.

## The Assumptive Close

### Assumptive vs. permission-seeking — what's the difference?

Assumptive language STATES what happens next, as something already decided:
"So we'll get you started today" / "I'll go ahead and get this bound with a
start date of the 1st." Permission-seeking language ASKS for the prospect's
approval to do the obvious next thing: "Would you like me to put together a
quote?" / "Is it okay if I go ahead and set that up?" Apollo's whole
purpose in tracking this (see METHODOLOGY.md's askq/asks) is to catch a
producer who works a call well and then loses it right at the end by asking
permission to finish what they already earned.

### Assumptive at the quote stage

Weak (permission-seeking): "Would you like me to put together a quote for
you?" Strong (assumptive): "Let me pull together your numbers — I'll have
them ready in just a minute." The difference: the second version treats the
quote as already happening, not as a favor waiting on approval.

### Assumptive at the close

Weak (permission-seeking): "Would you like to move forward with this
policy?" or "Is that something you'd want to do?" Strong (assumptive):
"I'll get this started with a policy date of the 1st — does that work, or
would the 15th be better?" The strong version assumes the sale and only asks
a detail question (which date), not whether the sale happens at all.

### Assumptive doesn't mean pushy

Being assumptive is about LANGUAGE, not pressure. "I'll go ahead and set
that up for you" said warmly, after real discovery and a real objection
handled, is assumptive. Talking over someone or refusing to take a real "no"
is not assumptive — it's just pushy, and it's the opposite of the trust an
assumptive close is built on.
